Each choice traces back to the brief, to something I counted on the current screens, or to published research. The brief’s user research is a single review thread, so these are hypotheses to test, not proof.

## Home first
The north star counts users who select or plan an outfit, and every session starts on Home. Today her suggestions start in the bottom sixth of the screen, under a friend’s outfit, and 57% of viewing time goes to what’s above the fold (NN/g, 2018). So the outfit and its button have to be on the first screen.

## One pick, two alternates
The brief’s users have limited mental bandwidth, and choice overload is worst under time pressure and uncertain preferences (Chernev et al., 2015). That evidence is mixed, so I kept two alternates and piece swaps rather than a single option.

## Plan chips and a reason line
Alta already knows her calendar and the weather, so tapping a chip replaces typing into a blank chat (recognition over recall, Nielsen, 1994). The reason under each pick shows the inputs the model used. Explanations raise acceptance of recommendations (Herlocker et al., 2000), without changing the AI.

## Saved looks and tomorrow’s plan on Home
On Day 3, Alex can’t find a saved look, so the right ones now come to her, filtered by today’s plan, instead of sitting in a calendar. Asking “Plan tomorrow?” right after she picks turns one choice into the next visit. Specific plans improve follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

## Shopping only from a real gap
Users complain that shopping competes for attention, yet Alex’s wardrobe is incomplete. Products appear only when a gap blocks a planned outfit, below the decision and labelled as partner items (FTC, 2015). “I have one” comes first, because she prefers to use what she owns.

## Add items: keep by default, ask only when unsure
Today each item has five controls, so a 20-item upload means about 100 decisions. Defaults reliably change behaviour: auto-enrolment raised 401(k) sign-up among new hires by 48 points (Madrian & Shea, 2001). So every detected item is kept, and only low-confidence items ask a one-tap question, which catches errors like the wrong brand “Mobbin”.
